An image to text converter takes a photo, screenshot or scan and gives back text you can search, edit or feed to a program. For a single screenshot, the tool built into your phone does the job. This guide is for the other case: hundreds or millions of images, arriving as phone photos of receipts, scanned forms, whiteboards and signs, where you need the text to come back the same way every time and land somewhere useful.

We compared eight converters that work at that scale: LlamaParse, Google Cloud Vision, Azure AI Vision, Amazon Textract, Mistral OCR, the vision models behind ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, Tesseract and OCR.space. The comparison runs on four things: what kind of image each handles well, what comes back with the text, how much you can convert for free, and what a thousand images cost. Prices are US list as of November 17, 2026.

Our pick is LlamaParse. It treats an image as the first step of agentic document processing: camera-photo correction flattens a tilted phone picture, OCR reads it in any of 80-plus languages, and the result comes back as Markdown, JSON or text with word positions, ready to become typed fields. On ParseBench-2, the public parsing benchmark LlamaIndex published on November 12, 2026, LlamaParse Agentic Plus scores 89.8 overall, ahead of every API and vision model ranked, and the free plan converts 10,000 images a month on the Fast tier.

How we chose these eight

We started from the questions people ask once a web converter stops being enough: which tool handles photos taken on a phone, which one reads handwriting, which returns structured output a program can use, and which image-to-text models are most accurate. A tool made the list if it converts common image formats to text through an API or a library, publishes its pricing or its free tier, and gets compared by name in those conversations.

LlamaParse is a LlamaIndex product. Its figures come from the published pricing page and documentation and from ParseBench-2, LlamaIndex's public benchmark of 41 parser, vision-model and open-weight configurations. The other tools' figures are their own published prices and documentation, and we say so where a vendor claim appears.

1. LlamaParse

ParseBench-2: 89.8 overall

LlamaParse accepts JPG, PNG, TIFF, WebP and HEIC images alongside PDFs and Office files, and it runs OCR on any page without a text layer. The image-specific piece is camera-photo correction: turn it on and a receipt photographed on a desk is found, cropped, perspective-corrected and relit before parsing, while clean scans and screenshots pass through untouched. OCR reads more than 80 languages, with a language list you can set when the primary language isn't English.

What comes back is the difference from a plain OCR call. Text returns as Markdown with tables intact, as JSON with word, line and cell bounding boxes, or as spatial text that keeps a form's alignment. Hand it a schema and the same pipeline returns typed fields with a confidence score and a citation to the spot on the image, at 95.6% F1 on ExtractBench. On ParseBench-2 the Agentic Plus tier scores 89.8 overall and 87.5 on visual grounding, against a 64.5 median for vision models called directly.

What you get

  • Four tiers priced in credits per image: Fast at 1, Cost Effective at 3, Agentic at 10, Agentic Plus at 25. Fast is OCR with no AI pass; Agentic is the recommended default for photos and scans.
  • Markdown, JSON and spatial text; word, line and cell bounding boxes on request; per-page screenshots and extracted image crops.
  • Camera-photo correction for phone pictures; OCR language hints for 80-plus languages; handwritten annotations read alongside print.
  • Extraction into typed fields with confidence and citations; classification and splitting in the same API.

Best for

  • Phone photos of receipts, forms and IDs that have to come back as fields, not just text.
  • Scans and screenshots where tables, columns and reading order matter downstream.
  • Agents and automations that call conversion as a tool through the hosted MCP server.

Watch out for

  • Pricing is in credits, not images. Fast is 1 credit an image; Agentic Plus is 25. Budget by tier.
  • It's a hosted service. Running in your own cloud is an Enterprise plan.
  • ParseBench-2 is LlamaIndex's own benchmark. The cases are public; run your own 50 images before you trust any number, including this one.

Integration and cost

  • Integration: REST API; SDKs in Python, TypeScript, Go and Java; CLI; webhooks; hosted MCP server at mcp.llamaindex.ai.
  • Runs in your network: hosted, with US and EU regions; deployment in your own cloud is an Enterprise plan.
  • Cost: pay as you go at $1.50 per 1,000 credits with 10,000 free credits a month, so Fast conversion is $1.50 per 1,000 images, Agentic $15 and Agentic Plus $37.50. Pro at $500 a month brings the rate to $1.25 per 1,000 credits. Details on the pricing page.

Here's the whole call in Python for a phone photo: upload, turn on camera-photo correction, tell OCR which languages to expect, and read Markdown back.

2. Google Cloud Vision

ParseBench-2: No public parsing benchmark result.

Google Cloud Vision is the image side of Google's OCR; Document AI is the document side. Two features matter here. Text Detection reads text anywhere in a photo, a street sign or a product label, and returns the full string plus each word with its bounding box. Document Text Detection is tuned for dense pages and handwriting, and returns pages, blocks, paragraphs, words and break markers. Both cost $1.50 per 1,000 images after the first 1,000 a month, dropping to $0.60 above five million.

It's the right call for an app that points a camera at the world: fast, synchronous, with automatic language detection and optional hints for the rest. The limits show on documents. Output is positioned text, not Markdown or fields, and once you need tables or key-value pairs you're into Document AI's processors at $10 to $30 per 1,000 pages.

What you get

  • Text Detection for scene text; Document Text Detection for dense pages and handwriting, with a BCP-47 handwriting hint.
  • JSON with blocks, paragraphs, words and bounding boxes; automatic language detection.
  • JPEG, PNG, GIF, BMP, WebP, RAW, ICO, PDF and TIFF up to 20 MB; async batches of up to 2,000 files from Cloud Storage.

Best for

  • Mobile and web apps that read signs, labels and screenshots in real time.
  • Google Cloud pipelines that land text in BigQuery at volume.

Watch out for

  • No Markdown, no tables and no typed fields; that's Document AI, priced separately.
  • Each feature applied to an image is a billable unit; run both detections and you pay twice.
  • Large images are resized to 75 megapixels; recommended input is 1024 by 768.

Integration and cost

  • Integration: REST and gRPC with client libraries in Python, Java, Node, Go and .NET; Cloud Storage input for batches.
  • Runs in your network: no. Cloud only.
  • Cost: $1.50 per 1,000 images for either detection, first 1,000 a month free, $0.60 above five million (Google's published pricing, November 17, 2026).

3. Azure AI Vision

ParseBench-2: No public parsing benchmark result.

Azure AI Vision's Read feature is Microsoft's image OCR, separate from Document Intelligence, which handles multi-page documents and forms. Read takes JPEG, PNG, BMP, TIFF or PDF, returns words, lines and paragraphs with positions and confidence scores, and handles mixed print and handwriting on the same image. Printed text covers Latin, Cyrillic, Arabic and Devanagari scripts plus Chinese, Japanese and Korean; handwriting covers nine languages.

The pricing is simple: $1.50 per 1,000 transactions on the standard tier, $0.60 above a million, and 5,000 free transactions a month on the free tier, the largest free allowance of the cloud OCR services here. It's a synchronous call built for user-facing features. If the images are really scanned documents with tables, Document Intelligence's Layout model is the better fit, and it's the one ParseBench-2 scores, at 66.1.

What you get

  • Words, lines and paragraphs with bounding polygons and confidence; mixed print and handwriting.
  • Images from 50 by 50 up to 10,000 by 10,000 pixels; 4 MB on the free tier, larger on standard.
  • Image Analysis 4.0 for synchronous calls; the Read container ships the v3.2 engine for on-premises use.

Best for

  • Microsoft-centred apps that need OCR inside a product feature with low latency.
  • Prototyping on the free tier before committing to a document pipeline.

Watch out for

  • Positioned text only; tables, selection marks and fields mean switching to Document Intelligence at $10 per 1,000 pages.
  • Free tier reads only the first two pages of a PDF or TIFF.
  • The container runs the older v3.2 engine, not Image Analysis 4.0.

Integration and cost

  • Integration: REST API and SDKs in Python, .NET, Java and JavaScript; Azure regions.
  • Runs in your network: partly. A Read container is available, on the v3.2 engine.
  • Cost: $1.50 per 1,000 transactions up to a million, $0.60 after; 5,000 free a month on F0 (Azure retail price list, East US, November 17, 2026).

4. Amazon Textract

ParseBench-2: No public parsing benchmark result.

Amazon Textract converts JPEG, PNG, TIFF and PDF into text blocks with geometry. DetectDocumentText is the image-to-text call: lines and words with bounding boxes and confidence at $1.50 per 1,000 pages. AnalyzeDocument adds Tables, Forms, Queries and Signatures as features you pay for per page. It reads printed text in more than 40 languages and handwriting in English only.

It fits when images already land in S3: a synchronous call for a single image, an asynchronous job with an SNS notification for multi-page files, IAM for access. The output is JSON blocks; turning it into Markdown or a record is your code. Images are capped at 10 MB and 10,000 pixels a side, and text needs to be at least 15 pixels tall to be read reliably.

What you get

  • Lines and words with bounding boxes and confidence from DetectDocumentText; tables, key-value pairs, query answers and signatures from AnalyzeDocument.
  • AnalyzeExpense for receipts and invoices; AnalyzeID for identity documents.
  • Sync for single images; async for PDFs and TIFFs up to 3,000 pages.

Best for

  • AWS pipelines with S3 triggers and Lambda, where the image is already in the bucket.
  • Receipts and forms with stable layouts where key-value output is the goal.

Watch out for

  • Handwriting is English only.
  • Feature pricing stacks: Forms is $50 per 1,000 pages on top of text.
  • The free tier lasts three months: 1,000 text pages and 100 analysis pages a month.

Integration and cost

  • Integration: boto3 or any AWS SDK; StartDocumentTextDetection plus SNS for async; AWS regions only.
  • Runs in your network: no. AWS regions only.
  • Cost: DetectDocumentText $1.50 per 1,000 pages ($0.60 above a million); Tables $15; Forms $50; Queries $15 (US West list, November 17, 2026).

5. Mistral OCR

ParseBench-2: 72.5 overall

Mistral OCR takes an image URL or a base64 image in PNG, JPEG, AVIF and other formats, or a PDF, and returns Markdown per page in a JSON envelope. Ask for blocks and you get bounding boxes; set the granularity and you get confidence scores at page, block or word level. Mistral prices it at $4 per 1,000 pages, or $2 through the batch API, and claims 170 languages. Those are Mistral's figures.

On ParseBench-2, Mistral OCR 4.1 with annotation scores 72.5 overall, above the cloud OCR services and 17 points behind the leader. For a team that wants Markdown out of multilingual photos and scans at a flat price with one model name to remember, mistral-ocr-latest, it's the simplest paid option here. Enterprise customers can run it as a self-hosted container.

What you get

  • Markdown per page with tables, extracted images as placeholders you can map back, headers and footers.
  • Block bounding boxes and page, block or word confidence on request.
  • Document annotation for schema-style extraction, priced separately; batch inference at half price.

Best for

  • Multilingual images and scans that need to end up as Markdown.
  • Teams that want one endpoint rather than a family of features.

Watch out for

  • No published free tier; you pay from the first image.
  • Size limits for images aren't published in the docs; test your largest files.
  • Older model names are being retired; pin to the latest alias or a dated version.

Integration and cost

  • Integration: REST API with Python and TypeScript SDKs; image_url or document_url inputs; batch API.
  • Runs in your network: yes, as a self-hosted container on enterprise plans.
  • Cost: $4 per 1,000 pages; $2 per 1,000 in batch; annotation extra (Mistral's published pricing, November 17, 2026).

6. Vision models: GPT, Gemini and Claude

ParseBench-2: 85.7 (Claude Opus 5.5), 84.2 (Gemini 4), 77.1 (GPT-5.6), called directly

Paste an image into ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude and ask for the text, and you'll usually get it. The same works through their APIs: send the image with a prompt and read the reply. For a handful of images a week this is the best image to text converter most people already have, and it handles messy handwriting, odd layouts and questions about the content in one step.

The problems start at volume. Pricing is per token, so cost depends on image size and reply length. Positions and confidence scores aren't in the response unless you prompt for them and check them yourself, and output can vary run to run. On ParseBench-2 the best direct vision model scores 85.7 overall, four points behind LlamaParse Agentic Plus, and the median score on visual grounding, the measure of whether a value can be traced back to its spot on the image, is 64.5 against 87.5.

What you get

  • Text, a summary or an answer in one call; strong on handwriting, context and unusual layouts.
  • Any instruction you can write: translate as you convert, pull three fields, describe the photo.
  • APIs with Python and TypeScript SDKs, plus the chat apps for one-offs.

Best for

  • Occasional conversions where you want to ask a question about the image at the same time.
  • Prototyping an extraction before you commit to a pipeline.

Watch out for

  • No fixed per-image price; large images and long replies cost more.
  • No native word coordinates or confidence, so there's nothing to route on or highlight.
  • Grounding is the weak dimension on ParseBench-2: median 64.5 across the 14 vision models ranked.

Integration and cost

  • Integration: each vendor's chat API with image inputs; batch endpoints at a discount.
  • Runs in your network: no, except through open-weight models you host yourself.
  • Cost: per input and output token at each vendor's published rate; no per-image list price.

7. Tesseract

ParseBench-2: No public parsing benchmark result.

Tesseract is the open-source OCR engine, now in its fifth major version under the Apache 2.0 licence. It runs on your machine, reads PNG, JPEG, TIFF and most other image formats, and ships trained data for more than 100 languages. Output is plain text, or hOCR, TSV, ALTO and PAGE when you need word positions, and it can write a searchable PDF.

It costs nothing per image, and for clean, high-contrast printed text it's accurate. It's also the tool that makes you do the preprocessing: deskew, binarise, crop and scale before you call it, because Tesseract won't. There's no handwriting support and no layout model, so photos, multi-column pages and tables are where it falls behind the paid options.

What you get

  • Plain text, hOCR, TSV, ALTO and PAGE output; searchable PDF; 100-plus languages.
  • Command-line tool, C++ library and wrappers in Python (pytesseract), Java, .NET and more.
  • Runs anywhere: laptop, container, air-gapped server.

Best for

  • Clean printed scans at any volume where zero per-image cost beats convenience.
  • Air-gapped or offline environments where no cloud call is allowed.

Watch out for

  • No handwriting, no layout reconstruction, no confidence routing beyond per-word scores you wire up yourself.
  • Phone photos need preprocessing before they read well.
  • You own the engine: updates, language packs and throughput are your problem.

Integration and cost

  • Integration: CLI and C++ API; pytesseract and other wrappers; OCRmyPDF for searchable PDFs.
  • Runs in your network: yes. It only runs in your network.
  • Cost: free and open source under Apache 2.0; you pay for the compute and the engineering.

8. OCR.space

ParseBench-2: No public parsing benchmark result.

OCR.space is the free image to text API most developers find first. The free key allows 25,000 requests a month at 500 a day per IP, with a 1 MB file limit, and accepts PNG, JPG, GIF, BMP and TIFF as a URL, upload or base64 string. Engine 2 covers 33 languages with auto-detection and copes with rotated text and noisy backgrounds; Engine 3 covers more than 200 languages and adds handwriting, Markdown tables and checkboxes, limited to 1,000 conversions a month on the free plan.

Output is JSON text, with word and line coordinates when you ask for the overlay, plus an optional searchable PDF. PRO is $30 a month for 300,000 requests at 5 MB. It's a per-request service for single images and short PDFs; it isn't built for structured fields, confidence-gated workflows or long scans.

What you get

  • JSON text; word and line coordinates with the overlay option; searchable PDF.
  • Engine 3 handwriting, Markdown tables and checkbox detection, capped on the free plan.
  • No file storage: processed and discarded, with the PDF link expiring in an hour.

Best for

  • Prototypes, hobby projects and low-volume image OCR with no account friction.
  • Single receipts and screenshots where a free key is the whole requirement.

Watch out for

  • 1 MB per request on free; multi-page scans need PRO PDF at $60 a month.
  • No typed fields and no benchmark result; Engine 3 is slower and its coordinates are less precise.
  • Free-tier searchable PDFs carry a watermark.

Integration and cost

  • Integration: REST (GET or POST) with an API key; community client libraries.
  • Runs in your network: yes. OCR.space Local is an installable version licensed through sales.
  • Cost: free for 25,000 requests a month; PRO $30 a month; PRO PDF $60; Enterprise from $999 (OCR.space's published pricing, November 17, 2026).

Choosing an image to text converter for your use case

What's the best image to text converter for photos taken on a phone?

A phone photo is the hardest input on this list: tilted, shadowed, with a desk around the edges. LlamaParse's camera-photo correction handles that before OCR runs, finding the document, cropping it, fixing the perspective and flattening the lighting, so a snapped receipt parses like a flat scan. Google Cloud Vision's Text Detection and Azure AI Vision's Read also read photos well and return each word with its position, which is what you want for a live camera feature. Tesseract needs you to do the straightening first.

Which image to text converter handles handwriting?

Handwriting support splits the list. LlamaParse reads handwritten annotations alongside print on its Agentic tiers. Azure AI Vision's Read handles handwriting in nine languages, Google's Document Text Detection takes a handwriting hint, and Textract reads handwriting in English only. OCR.space's Engine 3 adds handwriting on paid plans; Tesseract doesn't read it at all. The vision models are the wild card: strong on messy script, with no confidence score to tell you when they guessed. For forms specifically, see our guide to the best OCR for handwritten forms.

Which image to text AI tools return structured output?

Plain text is the floor; the useful question is what else comes back. LlamaParse returns Markdown with tables, JSON with bounding boxes, and typed fields with confidence and a citation when you give it a schema. Mistral OCR returns Markdown with optional blocks and confidence. The cloud OCR services return positioned words and lines; tables and key-value pairs mean moving up to their document products at $10 to $50 per 1,000 pages. Vision models return whatever you ask for, with nothing attached to tell you where it came from.

Which image-to-text models are most accurate?

ParseBench-2 compares both kinds of model on the same 4.3k cases. Among parsers, LlamaParse Agentic Plus leads at 89.8, with Mistral OCR 4.1 at 72.5 and Azure Document Intelligence at 66.1. Among vision models called directly, Claude Opus 5.5 scores 85.7, Gemini 4 84.2 and GPT-5.6 77.1. The gap widens on visual grounding, where Agentic Plus scores 87.5 and the vision-model median is 64.5. Run the same 50 of your own images through any two finalists before you decide.

Final thoughts

The converter that handles a clean screenshot is any of these. The one that holds up on a thousand phone photos of receipts is the one that corrects the image first, tells you where each value came from, prices in a unit you can forecast, and publishes a benchmark you can rerun.

LlamaParse is where we'd start: camera-photo correction, 80-plus OCR languages, Markdown or JSON or typed fields from one call, the top ParseBench-2 score, and 10,000 free credits a month to prove it on your images. Google Cloud Vision and Azure AI Vision win for live camera features inside their clouds, Textract when the images already sit in S3, Mistral on multilingual Markdown at a flat price, Tesseract when the budget is zero and the scans are clean, and OCR.space when a free key is the whole requirement.

Then test it the only way that counts: 50 of your worst images through each finalist, scored on the values your application needs, with a note of how many came back with a confidence score you could route on.

Is there a free image to text converter?

Yes, at three levels. For a single picture, Google Lens, Apple's Live Text and Microsoft OneNote copy text out of an image for free. For a program, LlamaParse's free plan converts 10,000 images a month on the Fast tier, OCR.space allows 25,000 requests, Azure AI Vision 5,000 transactions and Google Cloud Vision 1,000 images. For unlimited free conversion you run Tesseract yourself and give up layout, handwriting and support. Our best OCR software guide covers the desktop and open-source options in more depth.

Can ChatGPT do OCR?

Yes. Paste an image and ask for the text, and ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude will return it, handwriting included. Through their APIs the same works at a per-token price. What you don't get is word positions, a confidence score or a repeatable per-image cost, and on ParseBench-2 the best direct vision model scores 85.7 against LlamaParse's 89.8, with a median of 64.5 on visual grounding. Use them for one-offs and prototypes; use a converter with positions and confidence for anything you'll run at volume.

Is Google OCR free?

Partly. Google Lens and the text selection in Google Photos are free for personal use. Google Cloud Vision's Text Detection and Document Text Detection include the first 1,000 images a month free, then cost $1.50 per 1,000. Google Document AI's OCR processor has the same 1,000 free pages and the same rate; its layout and form processors cost $10 to $30 per 1,000 pages.

What's the difference between an image to text converter and an OCR API?

An image to text converter is any tool that gets text out of a picture, from a phone app to a library. An OCR API is the programmable version, with a per-page price, a documented response and limits you can plan around. Every tool on this list except the phone apps is an OCR API underneath; our guide to the best OCR API compares the document-focused services on the same terms.

How accurate are image to text converters on handwriting?

Less accurate than on print, and the gap depends on the tool. The cloud services publish language lists rather than accuracy figures: nine handwriting languages for Azure, English only for Textract. LlamaParse's Agentic tiers read handwritten annotations with print and attach a confidence score to each extracted field, so you can route uncertain values to a person. Whatever you choose, score it on your own samples; vendor handwriting demos are chosen to read well.
